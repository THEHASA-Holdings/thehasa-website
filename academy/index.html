Digital Master Artisan Model Academy

Your workshop is already a classroom. We help you teach.

The Academy prepares experienced artisans to train young people inside their own businesses: salons, tailoring shops, carpentry and welding workshops, garages and farms. You learn how to teach a trade step by step, learners earn as their skills grow, and THEHASA supports you with modules, supervision and the route to national assessment.

What the Academy does

Registers and verifies artisans

Experienced practitioners register online, show their business and their work, and THEHASA verifies them before they train anyone.

Prepares them to teach

Short modules, live classes and quizzes on competency-based training, supervising learners, safety, safeguarding and assessment preparation.

Keeps quality visible

Attendance, learner progress and supervision visits are recorded, so THEHASA and its partners see what is happening at every training location.

Why we built it

For three years THEHASA Holdings has run the Master Artisan Model: instead of building a training centre in every community, we prepare artisans who already run businesses near the young people who need skills. Learners train in a real workplace, and THEHASA handles the structure, supervision and assessment.

As the network grew, phone calls, WhatsApp and field visits could no longer keep every location consistent. The Academy brings preparation, records and supervision into one place, so more artisans can join without losing quality.

Who can join

Artisans

You are 18 or older, you practise a trade, and you run or work in a business where a young person can learn safely. A formal certificate helps but is not required to start: experience and good work count.

Partners

Development organisations, employers and programmes that need young people trained in practical skills can contract THEHASA to coordinate delivery through the Academy network.

What the Academy certificate means. Completing the Academy shows you finished THEHASA's preparation programme for Master Artisans. It is an internal THEHASA recognition. It is not a national trainer licence, and national assessment and certificates for learners come from the Uganda Vocational and Technical Assessment Board (UVTAB).

Why the Academy

Skills already live in our communities. We connect them to young people.

We should not have to build a new training institution everywhere a young person needs a practical skill. Experienced artisans and working businesses are already there. What has been missing is a system that links them to structured training, supervision, quality checks and national assessment. The Academy is that system.

The problems we work on, and how the Academy answers each one

The problem

Learners cannot afford to stop earning

Many young people already support a household. Months of unpaid training mean no food money, so they never start, or they drop out halfway.

Our answer

Learn as you earn: as learners gain each competence, the artisan engages them in paid customer work, so income grows with skill.

The problem

Training is far away

Vocational centres are concentrated in a few towns. A young person 60 or 70 kilometres away often cannot pay for transport, rent or time away from family.

Our answer

Training happens in a business near where the learner already lives, with a prepared Master Artisan.

The problem

Skills without proof

Many young people learn on the job but have no certificate, so employers and customers overlook what they can do.

Our answer

Learners follow national occupational standards, keep a record of their competences, and are prepared for UVTAB assessment.

The problem

Workshops left out of the system

Salons, tailoring shops, garages and workshops hold real expertise, but they are rarely part of formal skills training.

Our answer

Each verified business becomes a registered, supervised learning location in one connected network.

The problem

Experts who were never taught to teach

An artisan can be excellent at the trade and still unsure how to teach it step by step, keep records or prepare a learner for assessment.

Our answer

Short modules, live classes and quizzes prepare artisans to teach, and they keep learning after they qualify.

The problem

Coordination by phone does not scale

Running many locations through calls, WhatsApp and field visits makes standards, records and supervision hard to keep consistent.

Our answer

Registration, attendance, progress and supervision sit in one place, so THEHASA sees every location and visits where support is needed most.

The problem

A certificate is not yet a job

Finishing a course does not guarantee income. Young people need customers, contacts and business experience.

Our answer

Learners train inside working businesses, build the artisan's network, and are followed up into jobs, apprenticeships or their own businesses.

Learn as you earn

Training that pays while it teaches

In the Master Artisan Model, learning does not wait for a certificate. Once a learner can do a task to standard, the artisan engages them in real customer work on that task and pays them a fair, agreed share. The more competences they master, the more work they can take on, and the more they earn.

For learners

  • Income from early in the training, so they can stay to the end.
  • Real customers and real standards, not only practice pieces.
  • A track record and references before they finish.
  • Confidence that the trade can feed them.

For artisans

  • Extra hands for more orders as learners become productive.
  • A chance to see who is reliable before hiring an assistant.
  • Payment for training under partner programmes, alongside the work learners help deliver.
  • A stronger name in the community as a teacher of the trade.

For families and partners

  • Training that young mothers and household heads can actually afford to attend.
  • Fewer dropouts, because learners do not have to choose between food and skills.
  • Skills that are already tested in the market when the course ends.
  • A smoother step from training into work or a business.
How we keep it fair. Earning follows learning, never the other way round. Work and pay follow Uganda's labour laws, including the stricter rules for anyone under 18, the share they earn is agreed in writing and recorded, training time stays protected, work is supervised and safe, and every learner can report a problem to THEHASA directly. This follows the principles of the ILO Quality Apprenticeships Recommendation, 2023 (No. 208).

How it works

From your workshop to a recognised trade.

Six steps, shared between you and THEHASA.

  1. RegisterFill in the online form with your trade, experience, business and photos of your work. You get a reference number straight away.
  2. VerificationTHEHASA calls you, checks your business and your work, and confirms your place. Your Master Artisan Digital Passport is opened.
  3. PreparationYou join live classes on Zoom or Google Meet and work through the modules on your phone. Each module ends with a short quiz.
  4. Training learnersWhen a partner programme starts, learners are placed with you. You train them in your business, one module at a time, and record attendance and progress. As they master each task, you engage them in paid customer work, so they learn as they earn.
  5. SupervisionTHEHASA supervisors follow progress through the records, coach you by phone or video, and visit your workplace in person.
  6. Assessment and next stepsTHEHASA registers ready learners for national assessment with UVTAB through an approved centre, and supports graduates into work or their own business.

Module 1 of the preparation programme

Teaching a trade one competence at a time.

About 25 minutes to read, plus a 6-question quiz. You can read offline once this page has opened on your phone.

1. What competence means2. How a module is built3. Show, explain, practise, check4. Keeping records5. Ready for assessmentQuiz

1. What competence means

A learner is competent when they can do a real task in your workplace, to the standard a customer expects, safely, and more than once. Knowing about a task is not enough. Competence combines three things:

  • Skill: the hands can do it, for example cutting a pattern or welding a straight seam.
  • Knowledge: they understand why each step matters, which tool to use, and what can go wrong.
  • Attitude: they work safely, keep time, respect the customer and take care of tools.
Key idea. In competency-based training, time spent is not what counts. A learner moves on when they can show the competence, not when a set number of weeks has passed.

2. How a national training module is built

Your trade already has national training modules, developed under Uganda's competence-based education and training (CBET) system. Each one is built from the Occupational Profile, the description of what a person in that job actually does, and the same profile is the benchmark for assessment. You do not write the modules. You learn to deliver them well.

Every module has the same parts:

  • Module purpose: what the learner will be able to do when the module is finished.
  • Learning-Working Assignments (LWAs): real or simulated jobs, like small projects. In your business they are real work for real customers.
  • Practical Exercises (PEXs): the smaller tasks inside each LWA, practised one by one.
  • Related knowledge and theory: the background you explain and demonstrate while learners do the LWA.
  • Health, safety and environment: rules observed and shown during every LWA and PEX.
  • Average duration, tools and materials: the contact hours and the minimum equipment the module needs.
Example from the Pig Farmer modules. Module 2, Construct piggery farm structures, has three LWAs: make the plan, erect the structures, maintain them. The LWA "Erect the structure" contains PEXs such as clear the site, demarcate the site, raise the structure, roof it, and fix feeders and drinkers.

Hours in a module are contact time, not calendar time: one day is about 8 hours of learning and one month about 160 hours. Quick learners may need less, others more. A learner may repeat a practical exercise until they reach the competence.

Non-formal and modular. This is non-formal, modular skills training, usually three or six months depending on the trade. It is not formal vocational education. Learners finish module by module and can sit national modular assessment with UVTAB when they are ready.

3. Show, explain, practise, check

Most practical skills are taught best in four moves. Use them for every new task.

ShowDo the task yourself at normal speed, then slowly.
ExplainTalk through each step and why it matters, including safety.
PractiseThe learner does it while you watch and correct gently.
CheckThe learner does it alone. You judge it against the outcome.

Keep each piece of teaching short. Demonstrate one step, let the learner try it, then add the next. Praise what is right before correcting what is wrong, and never let a learner practise an unsafe method.

Learn as you earn. Once a learner can do a task to standard, engage them in real customer work on it and pay them the share you agreed in writing. Customer work must follow their training plan and be supervised. Learners are never unpaid workers, and earning never replaces learning time.

4. Keeping records

Records protect you, the learner and the programme. They show the partner and THEHASA that real training is happening. For each learner, record:

  1. Attendance, every day they come.
  2. Which module and outcome they worked on.
  3. When they first did a task with help, and when they did it alone.
  4. Photos of their work where possible, with their permission.
  5. Any problem: absence, safety incident or concern about their wellbeing.

You will record these in the Academy on your phone. If you do not have a smartphone, keep the THEHASA paper logbook, and your supervisor will enter it for you.

5. Getting learners ready for assessment

National assessment is done by UVTAB through an approved assessment centre, not by you. Your role is to make sure the learner is ready:

  • They have completed every Learning-Working Assignment and its Practical Exercises in the module, alone, more than once.
  • They can explain the safety rules and why each step matters.
  • Their records and portfolio of work are complete.
  • You and your THEHASA supervisor agree they are ready before they are registered.

Never promise a learner a certificate. Promise good training and honest preparation. The certificate is earned at assessment.
